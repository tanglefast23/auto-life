### Recruiting

Each tester must be someone who has **not** played this game, watched it being built, seen a screenshot, or heard it described in detail. Not Joe. Three is the minimum, not the target — recruit four if you can, because a void session is expensive.

Ask before recording anything: "I'd like to write down what you say and do while you play. Nothing gets published. Is that OK?" If they decline, take notes without direct quotes and mark the sheet accordingly — condition 3 gets harder to judge, so prefer a tester who consents.

### Joe's diagnostic run

After P5 verification, Joe runs the same session and answers the same questions.
This is useful product evidence but does not count toward the three fresh external
sessions. If it causes any code or content change, freeze and hash a new build before
tester 1. Do not let the recruited testers watch Joe's run.

### Observer setup

- A timer you can start silently at the moment control is granted.
- The recording sheet in §4, printed or open, one per tester.
- Sit where you can see the screen without leaning in. Do not sit where the tester can read your face while they decide.

## 2. What the observer may and may not say

This is the part that decides whether the results mean anything. "No coaching from the observer beyond what the game itself says" (§6) is a hard rule, and testers *will* ask you things.

### The opening — say this, and nothing more

> "This is a life-simulation game. You're going to be in control of what the character does. I'm not going to explain how it works, because what I need to learn is what the game explains on its own. Play however you like, and please think out loud — say what you're noticing, what you're trying, what you expect to happen. If you ask me a question I'll probably not answer it, and that's not you doing badly, it's the test working."

Then start the build. Start the timer the moment queue control becomes interactive.

### Permitted responses to questions

A tester asking "what does this do?" is data, not an interruption. Log the question verbatim, then use one of these:

| Situation | Say exactly |
|---|---|
| Any question about what something does or means | "What do you think it does?" |
| They press for a real answer | "I know — I can't tell you. What would you guess?" |
| They ask if they did something wrong | "There's no wrong. Do what makes sense to you." |
| They ask whether to keep going | "Up to you." |
| They go quiet for ~30 seconds | "What are you thinking?" |
| They get stuck and frustrated | Say nothing for 60 seconds. Then: "What are you looking for?" |

### Never

- Never name a UI element the game hasn't named to them.
- Never point, hover, or lean toward the screen.
- Never say "try…", "you could…", "have you noticed…", or "good".
- Never explain a mechanic after they've struggled — not even once the session ends, until the debrief in §5.
- Never answer "did I pass?"

If you break one of these, write it on the sheet. A session with one logged slip is still usable; a session with an unlogged slip is worthless because you will not remember which observation it contaminated.

## 3. Session shape

1. **Opening script**, then hand over control. **Start the timer.**
2. **Observe** from control through at least the first recap. Write continuously; do not wait for interesting moments.
3. **At the first recap**, let them read it. Do not prompt. Note whether they mention anything from it unprompted.
4. **Offer one more day** with exactly: "There's another day if you want it. Want to keep going, or stop here?" Log the answer verbatim before anything else — this is condition 6 and the wording matters.
5. If they continue, observe the extra day the same way.
6. **Debrief** (§5).

### Condition evidence

Fill these from the log after the session, not during. If the log doesn't support it, it didn't happen.

|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Made a meaningful queue choice within **60 seconds** of receiving control | Y / N | Time: ____ · What they did: |
| 2 | Could point to what they expected one edit to change, **then** observed a visible consequence within ~3 minutes | Y / N | Expectation (verbatim): <br>Consequence seen at: ____ |
| 3 | Stated, **unprompted**, one decision they made and why | Y / N | Verbatim: |
| 5 | Noticed at least one lasting change or reward by the first recap | Y / N | Verbatim: |
| 6 | Chose to play the extra day **and** could say what they wanted to try or see | Y / N | Answer (verbatim): <br>What they wanted: |

Conditions 4 is cross-session — see §6 below.

### Judging calls

These are the three places observers reliably disagree with themselves a week later. Decide them the same way every time:

- **"Meaningful queue choice" (1)** — adding, removing, reordering, or pinning a card, done deliberately. Opening the palette and closing it is not a choice. Clicking an object that happens to queue something *is* a choice if they then look at the queue. Hovering is never a choice. If you are unsure, it is not a choice.
- **"Unprompted" (3)** — they said it without you asking anything in the preceding 30 seconds. "What are you thinking?" is a prompt: anything said in response to it does not count for condition 3, though it still goes in the log.
- **"Visible consequence" (2)** — a change *they noticed*, not one that occurred. If the bar moved and they said nothing and their eyes were elsewhere, condition 2 is not met.

### Observer slips

Log any coaching, pointing, or leading question you gave, with the timestamp. Then note which condition it might have contaminated.

## 5. Debrief (after the offer in §3.4 is logged)

Only after logging the continue/stop answer. Ask in this order, and write verbatim answers:

1. "Talk me through what you were trying to do."
2. "Was there anything you wanted to do and couldn't figure out how?"
3. "Was there anything that happened that you didn't expect?"
4. "If you played again tomorrow, what would you do differently?"

Then, and only then, answer any questions they asked during the session. They earned it.

## 6. Across all sessions

Two conditions are judged across the set, not per tester. Fill this once, after the last session.

| # | Condition (master §6 — locked) | Met? | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | At least **two distinct viable approaches** appeared across sessions (e.g. protecting Practice vs protecting bars), and neither created an unexplained dead end | Y / N | Approach A (tester, what they did): <br>Approach B: <br>Dead ends seen: |
| 6 | At least **2 of 3** chose the extra day and could say what they wanted to try | Y / N | Count: __ / __ |

**"Distinct viable approaches" (4)** — two testers pursuing recognisably different priorities, both of which the game let them carry out. Two testers doing the same thing with different card order is one approach. An approach that ran into a wall the game never explained fails the second half of this condition even if the first half is met.

## 7. Result

**P4.5 passes only if every per-tester condition is met by every tester, and both cross-session conditions are met.** There is no partial pass and no "close enough". Master §6: P6 stays closed until it passes.

### If it fails

For each failed condition, classify the cause as exactly one of — these six are from §6 and the list is closed:

`discoverability` · `comprehension` · `agency` · `consequence visibility` · `pacing` · `desire-to-continue`

Then:

1. Write the failure record: which conditions failed, for which testers, the classification, and the log evidence.
2. Change the **smallest implicated P2–P5 behaviour**. Not a redesign — the smallest change that plausibly addresses the classification.
3. Record the change and why it follows from the classification.
4. Freeze a new build with a new hash.
5. Rerun **this protocol unchanged**, with **fresh testers**. Testers who have already played are burned and cannot be reused.

Criteria cannot be weakened, waived, or moved without Joe's explicit decision (§6). If the same condition fails twice with different fixes, that is a signal about the design, not about the criterion.
